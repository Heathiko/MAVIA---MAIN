# Topics 15 and 19: concepts with full text

## The Human Organ System at Work (topic 15)

1. What I Need to Know (concept 169)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] Think of a basketball team. Each player has a job, but they must work together to win. Your organs are like that too! In this module, you will learn how your organs work as a team. After going through this module, you are expected to: 1. tell what an organ system is; 2. name the organ systems and the organs in each one;

2. Remember (concept 176)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] cells make tissues, and tissues make organs. When organs work together to do one big job, they form an organ system. Organ systems together make up the whole human body. No organ system works alone. Let us look at four examples of organ teamwork.

The left column lists the names of seven distinct organ systems, the middle column lists the specific organs that make up each system, and the right column describes the primary function of that system. The rows are arranged sequentially from top to bottom, starting with the skeletal system and ending with the nervous system. 4. Teamwork 1 (concept 178)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] The Heart and Lungs Every part of your body needs oxygen. The lungs get the oxygen, and the heart delivers it. Figure 1. How the heart and lungs work together

5. Teamwork 1: The Heart and Lungs - figure (concept 179)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] Picture description for Figure 1: The picture shows four boxes arranged in a circle. Arrows go from one box to the next, going around and around like a loop. Box 1, at the top: The lungs breathe in oxygen. The oxygen passes into the blood. Box 2, on the right: The heart pumps this oxygen-rich blood to all body parts. Box 3, at the bottom: The body parts use the oxygen and make a waste gas called carbon dioxide. Box 4, on the left: The blood carries the carbon dioxide back. The heart pumps it to the lungs, and the lungs breathe it out. Then the loop starts again at Box 1. This happens with every breath and every heartbeat. That is why, during exercise, both your heart and lungs work faster. Your muscles need more oxygen, so you breathe faster and your heart pumps faster.

   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] Getting Energy from Food Your body gets energy and nutrients from food. Many organs share this job. Esophagus: the food pipe pushes food down. Stomach: mixes food with juices. Small intestine: nutrients pass into the blood. Large intestine: takes back water. Anus: solid waste leaves the body. On the right side, an arrow goes from the small intestine box to a box labeled Heart. The heart pumps the blood carrying nutrients to all body parts. Below the heart box, an arrow goes down to a box labeled Kidneys. The kidneys clean the blood and make urine. Tactile tip: Lay a long piece of yarn on a table to show the path of food. Tie a knot for each organ. Tie a second piece of yarn at the small intestine knot to show nutrients going to the heart. Here, the digestive system breaks down food. The circulatory system carries nutrients to the body. The urinary system removes waste from the blood. Three systems work as one team!

8. Teamwork 3 (concept 182)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] The Brain, the Boss of the Body The brain is the control center. Thin threads called nerves carry messages between the brain and every body part. The brain, spinal cord, and nerves make up the nervous system.

9. Teamwork 3: The Brain, the Boss of the Body - figure (concept 183)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] Picture description for Figure 3: In the center of the picture is a box labeled Brain, control center. Four boxes are around it, one in each corner. Top left: Eyes and ears. An arrow points from this box to the brain. They send messages to the brain. Top right: Muscles. An arrow points from the brain to the muscles. The brain tells them to move. Example: You hear your friend call your name. Your ears send a message to your brain. Your brain understands the message and tells your neck muscles to turn your head and your mouth to answer. 10. Teamwork 4 (Part 1 of 3) (concept 184)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] Bones and Muscles Bones cannot move by themselves. Muscles are attached to bones. When a muscle pulls, the bone moves. The skeletal system and the muscular system work together every time you walk, write, or play.
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] Think of Carlos Yulo, the Filipino gymnast who won gold medals in gymnastics. Every flip and twist he makes needs his bones and muscles working together. His brain controls each move, and his heart and lungs give his muscles oxygen.
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] Try it: Hold your right arm straight out. Put your left hand on the front of your upper right arm. Now bend your right elbow and bring your hand to your shoulder. Feel the muscle get hard and bunch up. It is pulling your arm bone up!

11. When One Organ Does Not Work Well (concept 187)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] Because organs work as a team, a problem in one organ affects the others. If the lungs are harmed by smoke, less oxygen gets into the blood. The heart must work harder, and the body feels tired. If the heart stops pumping, oxygen and nutrients cannot reach the brain and other organs. If the kidneys do not work well, waste stays in the blood and can make a person very sick.

12. This is why we must take care of all our organs (concept 188)
   - [Science4_Human_Organ_Systems_at_Work] This is why we must take care of all our organs: eat healthy food, drink clean water, exercise, sleep enough, and stay away from smoke.

## Reproduction Among Flowering Plants (topic 19)

1. The main parts of a flower involved in reproduction (concept 129)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] The flower is arranged in distinct layers, with a small, pointed sepal at the very base, followed by four large, rounded petals that form the outer ring. Inside the petals, two stamens stand on either side of the central structure, each consisting of a slender filament topped by a small, oval anther. In the very center is the pistil, which features a bulbous ovary at the bottom containing three small, round ovules, a long, narrow style rising from it, and a flat, disc-shaped stigma at the very top.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] The flower is arranged with five broad petals radiating evenly around a central core, while two smaller, pointed sepals sit at the base of the stem. In the very center, a vertical structure called the pistil rises from a bulbous ovary, which contains a small ovule, up to a rounded stigma at the top. To the right of the pistil, a separate male structure called the stamen stands slightly shorter, consisting of a thin filament supporting an oval anther.

2. Reproduction in Flowering Plants (concept 130)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] Flowering plants reproduce sexually, which means a new plant begins when a male sex cell joins a female sex cell. Most of this activity happens inside the flower, which is the reproductive part of the plant. A single flower can contain both male and female parts, and these parts work together so that seeds can form. Seeds later grow into new plants, allowing the species to continue.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] Flowering plants reproduce sexually, which means a new plant begins when a male sex cell joins a female sex cell. Most of this activity happens inside the flower, which is the reproductive part of the plant.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] A single flower can contain both male and female parts, and these parts work together so that seeds can form. Seeds later grow into new plants, allowing the species to continue. Male parts (the stamen) produce pollen; female parts (the pistil) hold the egg cells.

3. Stamen (Male Part) (concept 258)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] The stamen is made up of the anther and the filament. The filament is the thin stalk that holds up the anther, while the anther produces tiny grains called pollen. Each pollen grain carries a male sex cell.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] The stamen is made up of the anther and the filament. The filament is the thin stalk that holds up the anther, while the anther produces tiny grains called pollen. Each pollen grain carries a male sex cell.

4. Pistil (Female Part) (concept 259)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] The pistil, also called the carpel, is made up of the stigma, style, and ovary. The sticky stigma catches pollen, the style connects the stigma to the ovary, and the ovary contains ovules. Each ovule holds a female sex cell, or egg.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] The pistil, also called the carpel, is made up of the stigma, style, and ovary. The sticky stigma catches pollen, the style connects the stigma to the ovary, and the ovary contains ovules. Each ovule holds a female sex cell, or egg.

5. Petals and Sepals (Supporting Parts) (concept 260)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] The colorful petals attract pollinators such as bees, butterflies, and birds. The green sepals protect the flower while it is still a bud. These parts do not make sex cells, but they help reproduction succeed.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] The colorful petals attract pollinators such as bees, butterflies, and birds. The green sepals protect the flower while it is still a bud. These parts do not make sex cells, but they help reproduction succeed.

6. Pollination (concept 134)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] Pollen is carried from the anther to the stigma. This can happen within the same flower or between different flowers, with the help of wind, water, insects, or other animals.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] Pollen is carried from the anther to the stigma. This can happen within the same flower or between different flowers, with the help of wind, water, insects, or other animals.

7. Fertilization (concept 261)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] After landing on the stigma, a pollen grain grows a tube down the style into the ovary. The male sex cell then joins the egg cell inside an ovule.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] After landing on the stigma, a pollen grain grows a tube down the style into the ovary. The male sex cell then joins the egg cell inside an ovule.

8. Seed formation (concept 262)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] The fertilized ovule develops into a seed, which contains a tiny young plant and a store of food.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] The fertilized ovule develops into a seed, which contains a tiny young plant and a store of food.

9. Fruit formation (concept 263)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] The ovary grows into a fruit that surrounds and protects the seeds and helps them spread to new places.
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-1] The ovary grows into a fruit that surrounds and protects the seeds and helps them spread to new places.

10. Everyday Examples (concept 138)
   - [Reproduction-Among-Flowering-Plants-2] A gumamela (hibiscus) shows all the flower parts clearly, which is why it is often used in class. A mango tree flowers, is pollinated, and then forms the mango fruit with a seed inside. Rice plants are pollinated mostly by wind, while many fruit trees depend on bees. These examples show that pollination and fertilization must happen first before a seed or fruit can form, and each seed carries the beginnings of a brand-new plant.
