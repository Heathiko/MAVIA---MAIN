# Topics 3 and 10: concepts with full text

## Grouping Materials Based on Properties (topic 3)

1. The three classification tests specified in the Grade 4 competency (concept 116)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] The figure shows three separate experiments designed to classify materials. The first experiment displays two materials: absorbent cloth, paper, and sponge on the left, and non-absorbent plastic, glass, and metal on the right. The second experiment shows a wooden and plastic float alongside a stone and metal sink, with the materials placed in water to observe whether they float or sink. Finally, the third experiment presents two groups of materials: decaying leaves, food scraps, paper, and wood on one side, and materials that do not decay, such as plastic, glass, metal, and rubber, on the other.

2. Properties of Materials (Part 1 of 3) (concept 117)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] Every material can be described by the way it looks, feels, and behaves. These descriptions are its properties. When learners in the elementary grades sort materials, they are not memorizing lists — they are using a property as a rule for putting things into groups.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] This skill is introduced in Grade 3 with the physical properties of solid materials, and is extended in Grade 4 into three specific classification tests that learners can actually perform in the classroom. Physical properties can be observed without changing what the material is made of.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] The Grade 3 competency names three that learners can check by hand: whether a material is hard (a stone, a coin), shiny (a new spoon, aluminum foil, a tansan), or stretchable (a rubber band, a garter, a balloon). Other familiar physical properties are color, texture, size, weight, and flexibility. Chemical properties describe how a material behaves when it reacts or changes into something new.

3. The Grade 4 competency names three (concept 120)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] The Grade 4 competency names three: whether a material can be burned, whether it reacts with other materials, and whether it is degradable or biodegradable. These properties cannot be seen at a glance; they show themselves only when the material is exposed to fire, air, moisture, or other substances.

4. Ability to absorb water. Place a drop of water on the material and watch what happens. If the (concept 121)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] water soaks in and the material becomes damp, the material is absorbent. If the water stays on the surface as a bead and can be wiped off, the material is non-absorbent. Cloth, paper, sponge, cotton, and wood absorb water; plastic, glass, metal, and rubber do not.

5. Ability to float or sink. Place the material gently in a basin of water. Materials that stay on the (concept 122)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] surface float; materials that go down to the bottom sink. Dry wood, plastic bottle caps, styrofoam, and dry leaves float. Stone, coins, nails, and marbles sink. Learners often expect size to decide this, so it is worth showing them a large piece of wood that floats and a small nail that sinks.

6. Ability to undergo decay. Materials that rot, break down, and return to the soil over time are said (concept 123)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] to undergo decay, or to be biodegradable. Food scraps, leaves, paper, and wood decay. Plastic, glass, metal, and rubber do not decay within a useful length of time, so they are called non- biodegradable. This test cannot be finished in one class period; it is usually set up as a week-long or month-long observation using two buried samples.

7. Grouping the Same Materials in Different Ways (concept 124)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] There is no single correct way to group a set of materials. The grouping depends on which property is being used as the rule. Consider six items commonly available in a Filipino classroom: Reading the table down each column gives a different pair of groups each time. The basahan joins the dried leaf and the wood under one rule, but joins the plastic bag under another. This is the central idea of the lesson: a material has several properties at once, and the group it belongs to changes with the question being asked.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] The table shows six different materials arranged in three columns. The first column lists the materials: basahan, plastic sando bag, dried leaf, tansan, piece of wood, and marble. The second column describes how each material interacts with water, indicating whether it absorbs water, floats, or sinks. Finally, the third column details whether each material undergoes decay. The basahan floats at first then sinks, and if made of cotton, it does undergo decay. The plastic sando bag floats and does not decay, while the dried leaf floats and does decay.

8. Why Grouping Matters (Part 1 of 3) (concept 126)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] Use, Handling, and Disposal People choose materials for a purpose because of their properties. A raincoat and a payong are made of plastic or coated cloth because those materials do not absorb water. A bimpo and a basahan are made of cotton because cotton does.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] A banca is made of wood because wood floats. Knowing the property explains the choice. Grouping also decides how waste should be handled, which is the point of the Grade 3 competency on proper disposal. Biodegradable waste such as vegetable peelings and fallen leaves can be composted and returned to the garden as fertilizer.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] Non-biodegradable waste such as plastic bottles, tin cans, and glass must be cleaned and set aside for recycling, because it will not break down on its own. This is the science behind the familiar nabubulok and di-nabubulok labels on segregation bins in schools and barangays.

## Separating Mixture (topic 10)

1. Each technique takes advantage of a different property of the components (concept 84)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] The figure shows four different methods for separating mixtures. First, there’s decantation, which shows a liquid being poured off, with heavier solids sinking to the bottom. Second, filtering demonstrates a container with a filter that traps solids but allows the liquid to pass through. Third, evaporation is depicted with water leaving as vapor, and the dissolved solid is left behind. Finally, scooping illustrates a floating layer being lifted with a spoon.

2. Why Mixtures Can Be Separated (concept 85)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] Because the components of a mixture are only physically combined, each one keeps its own properties.

3. Picking is the simplest method. The components differ in appearance and are large enough to be (concept 86)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] handled one at a time. Removing small stones from rice before cooking, taking the bones out of fried fish, and sorting good mongo seeds from damaged ones are all examples of picking.

4. Sieving separates solids of different particle sizes using a sieve, strainer, or screen. The finer (concept 87)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] particles fall through the holes while the larger ones stay behind. Sifting flour to remove lumps, screening sand at a construction site, and straining brewed tea leaves from the liquid all use sieving.

5. Winnowing separates light components from heavy ones using moving air. Farmers toss threshed (concept 88)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] rice into the air using a bilao; the wind carries away the light husks or ipa while the heavier grains fall back into the tray. The same principle is used when separating chaff from mongo or corn.

6. Scooping removes a component that has floated to the surface. Skimming the fat from a pot of (concept 89)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] sabaw, lifting oil off the top of water, and gathering leaves from the surface of a pail all use scooping.

7. Decantation separates a liquid from a solid that has settled, or two liquids that do not mix. The (concept 90)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] mixture is left to stand until the heavier component sinks, then the upper liquid is poured off carefully without disturbing the layer below. Rinsing rice, letting muddy water settle before pouring off the clear part, and separating oil from water all use decantation.

8. Filtering uses a filter paper, cloth, or fine mesh through which the liquid passes while the solid is (concept 91)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] trapped. It catches particles too fine to remove by decantation. Brewing coffee through a cloth strainer, straining gata from grated coconut, and using a household water filter are all examples.

9. Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid from a solution by heating the liquid or leaving it under the (concept 92)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] sun until the liquid escapes as vapor, leaving the solid behind. This is exactly how asin is produced in the salt beds of Pangasinan and other coastal provinces: seawater is led into shallow ponds, the sun evaporates the water, and salt crystals remain.

10. Using a magnet separates iron or steel from other materials, because only certain metals are (concept 93)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] attracted to magnets. A magnet passed over a mixture of iron filings and sand lifts out the iron and leaves the sand. Junk shops and recycling facilities use large magnets to pull steel out of mixed scrap.

11. Choosing the Right Technique (concept 94)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] Some mixtures need more than one technique used in order. To recover both the salt and the sand from a mixture of salt, sand, and water, a learner would first filter the mixture to trap the sand, then evaporate the filtered liquid to recover the salt. Planning the correct sequence is the most demanding part of this competency.

12. Choosing the Right Technique (concept 95)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] The figure shows a table detailing how to separate different mixtures. The first entry describes rice mixed with small stones, where the technique used is picking, indicating the stones are noticeably different in appearance and size compared to the rice. The second entry shows flour with lumps, and the best technique to use is sieving, which suggests the lumps are larger than the normal particle size of the flour. The third entry illustrates palay with ipa husks, and the appropriate method is winnowing, signifying that the weight of the husks is different from the palay. The fourth entry details sabaw with fat on top, where the fat floats, and scooping is the technique used to separate it. The fifth entry describes sand and water, where the sand settles, and decantation is the method employed.

13. Benefits of Separating Mixtures (Part 1 of 2) (concept 96)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] Separation is not a classroom exercise only; it is how many useful products are made. It gives us clean drinking water from cloudy sources, salt from seawater, gata from grated coconut, cooking oil from crushed seeds, and clean rice ready for the pot.
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] It allows recyclable metal to be recovered from mixed waste, and it is the reason a school can turn segregated waste into something valuable. In every case, separating a mixture takes something useful out of something that was not yet usable.
