# Topics 5 and 7: concepts with full text

## Mixtures and Their Characteristics (topic 5)

1. Solutions, colloids, and suspensions differ in appearance and in how they treat light (concept 72)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] The illustration shows three distinct types of mixtures. The first, labeled “Solution,” depicts a clear, uniform liquid where light passes straight through, and particles are too small to be seen or settle. The second, “Colloid,” shows a cloudy mixture where light is scattered inside, but the particles do not separate when the mixture is left undisturbed, like gatas, taho, or mayonesa. Finally, the third, “Suspension,” is a cloudy mixture with solid particles at the bottom that settle out over time, such as muddy water or gawgaw in water, and light is blocked.

2. Uniform and Non-Uniform Mixtures (Part 1 of 3) (concept 73)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] The Grade 6 competency asks learners to sort mixtures by appearance. The question is simple: does A uniform mixture, also called a homogeneous mixture, looks the same in every part. The components are so evenly spread that you cannot tell them apart by looking.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] A glass of sugar water, a cup of brewed coffee without grounds, and a sip of buko juice all look like a single liquid, even though each one contains several materials. Uniform mixtures of this kind are called solutions. A non-uniform mixture, also called a heterogeneous mixture, shows its components.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] You can see the separate parts, and different spoonfuls may not look the same. A bowl of halo-halo, a plate of pancit, a handful of rice mixed with corn, and a glass of muddy water are all non-uniform.

3. Solutions: Solute and Solvent (concept 76)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] In a solution, the material that dissolves is the solute, and the material that does the dissolving is the solvent. In sweetened coffee, the sugar is the solute and the hot water is the solvent. The solvent is usually the component present in the greater amount, and water is so common as a solvent that it is often called the universal solvent. Three conditions affect how quickly a solute dissolves, and learners can test each one: Size of the particles. Finely ground sugar dissolves faster than a large lump, because more of its surface touches the water. Stirring. A stirred glass dissolves its sugar faster than one left standing, because stirring keeps bringing fresh water into contact with the solute. Temperature. Sugar dissolves faster in hot water than in cold water, which is why powdered drinks mix easily in warm water but leave grains at the bottom of a cold glass. Not every solute dissolves in every solvent. Salt and sugar dissolve in water, but cooking oil does not. Oil and water separate into two layers no matter how long they are stirred, which is why a bottle of salad dressing must be shaken before use.

4. Suspensions and Colloids (Part 1 of 3) (concept 77)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Between the clear solution and the obviously separated mixture are two kinds worth naming. A suspension is a non-uniform mixture in which solid particles are large enough to be seen and heavy enough to settle when the mixture is left alone.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Gawgaw stirred into water, muddy water from a flooded street, and a bottle of medicine labelled "shake well before using" are all suspensions. Their particles can be trapped by filter paper. A colloid sits in between.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Its particles are too small to settle out, so the mixture stays evenly cloudy for a long time, but they are still large enough to scatter light. 5. Air as a Mixture (concept 80)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Not all mixtures are liquids. Air is a mixture of gases, and the Grade 6 competency names its main components: nitrogen, which makes up the largest share; oxygen, which living things need for respiration; carbon dioxide, which plants use to make food; and water vapor, whose amount rises and falls with the weather.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Air is a uniform mixture: it looks the same throughout, and its gases do not settle into layers. Because the components are only physically combined, each keeps its own properties — which is why oxygen can still support burning even while it is mixed with the other gases.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] The figure shows six different mixtures, each listed with its components, appearance, and type. The first mixture, kape with sugar, displays a clear, uniform appearance, meaning the coffee, sugar, and water are evenly mixed throughout. In contrast, the halo-halo shows distinct parts – ice, milk, fruits, and beans – clearly visible within the mixture. The gatas mixture presents as evenly cloudy, and the droplets do not settle, classifying it as a colloid, which is non-uniform. The gawgaw in water appears cloudy and settles when standing, indicating it’s a suspension, also non-uniform. The air mixture is invisible and appears the same throughout, demonstrating it’s a uniform mixture.

## Changes that Materials Undergo (topic 7)

1. Changes in solid materials when bent, pressed, hammered, and cut (concept 99)
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] The image shows four distinct ways a solid material can be altered. The first illustration depicts a shape changing when a material is bent, but the size remains the same. The second shows a material being pressed, resulting in a flattened shape and the original amount of material remaining unchanged. The third illustrates a material being hammered, where the shape and thickness change, and the metal itself remains solid. Finally, the fourth shows a material being cut, resulting in multiple pieces, each retaining the same original material.

2. Bending changes the shape of a material while its size stays the same. A wire, a plastic straw, a (concept 100)
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] paper clip, and a strip of cardboard can all be bent. Materials that bend without breaking are flexible; materials such as chalk, glass, and dry twigs are rigid and snap instead.

3. Pressing squeezes a material and flattens it. Dough pressed by the palm spreads out and becomes (concept 101)
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] wider and thinner, though the amount of dough has not changed. A tin can stepped on during a game of tumbang preso becomes crumpled: its shape changes while the metal remains metal.

4. Hammering delivers a stronger, sharper force. A piece of soft metal that is hammered becomes (concept 102)
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] flatter and wider, which is how a panday shapes a bolo or a tansan is flattened into a washer. Brittle materials such as stone and glass do not flatten when hammered; they break into pieces.

5. Cutting divides a material into smaller parts, so the change is mainly in size. Paper cut with scissors (concept 103)
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] becomes several smaller papers. Cloth cut by a mananahi becomes the pieces of a garment. Each piece is still made of exactly the same material as before.

6. Changes Caused by Heat and by Oxygen (Part 1 of 3) (concept 104)
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] The Grade 5 competency moves from force to two conditions that cause deeper changes: application of heat and the presence or lack of oxygen. Heat can cause a material to melt, evaporate, soften, harden, or burn. Ice left on a saucer melts into water.
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] Candle wax melts and then hardens again when it cools. Sugar heated in a pan turns brown and gives off a sweet smell as it becomes caramel — and that change cannot be undone. Oxygen is part of the air around us, and many changes depend on whether it is present.
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] Iron exposed to air and moisture forms a reddish-brown coating called rust, which is why a bakal na pako left outdoors during the rainy season looks nothing like a new one. A candle covered by a glass jar goes out because the oxygen inside is used up. Sliced saging or mansanas turns brown when left on the table because its exposed flesh reacts with the oxygen in the air.

7. Reversible and Irreversible Changes (concept 107)
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] By Grade 6, learners sort changes into two kinds. A physical change alters only the appearance of a material — its size, shape, or state — while the material itself stays the same.
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] Physical changes are usually reversible: melted ice can be frozen again, a crumpled paper can be flattened, and dissolved salt can be recovered by evaporating the water. A chemical change produces an entirely new material with new properties.
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] Chemical changes are irreversible: rust cannot be turned back into shiny iron, burnt wood cannot be turned back into wood, and a cooked egg cannot be uncooked. Useful clues that a chemical change has taken place are a new color, a new smell, bubbles of gas, heat given off, or a solid forming where there was none.
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] The figure shows a table presenting different changes and how they are classified. The first row details four physical changes: bending a wire, which is caused by force and results in a physical change that is reversible; cutting paper, also caused by force, is a physical change that is not reversible; melting ice, caused by heat, is a physical change that is reversible; and burning wood into charcoal, caused by heat and oxygen, is a chemical change that is irreversible. The final row describes a chemical change – the rusting of a nail – caused by oxygen and moisture, and is also irreversible.
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] Oxygen and moisture Chemical No
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] Heat and water Chemical No

8. Useful and Harmful Changes (Part 1 of 3) (concept 113)
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] The last Grade 4 competency asks learners to judge a change, not just describe it. Many changes are useful. Cutting cloth produces clothing. Hammering metal produces tools. Cooking makes food safe and easier to digest.
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] Decaying leaves in a compost pit become fertilizer for a school garden. Other changes are harmful, especially to the environment. Rust weakens roofing sheets and bridges. Spoiled food causes illness.
   - [Lesson_Changes-that-Materials-Undergo] Burning plastic and other household waste releases smoke and toxic gases into the air, which is why the practice is discouraged and is covered by the Ecological Solid Waste Management Act. Learning to tell useful changes from harmful ones is what turns this lesson from a list of observations into a habit of responsible decision-making.
