# Topics 340 and 341: concepts with full text

## Solid, Liquid and Gas (topic 340)

1. Matter usually exists in one of three everyday states
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Matter usually exists in one of three everyday states: solid, liquid, and gas. What state matter is in depends on two things: how closely packed its particles are, and how much energy (movement) those particles have.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. Your desk, the air you breathe, the water you drink, and even you are all made of matter. Scientists sort matter into three common states based on how its particles are arranged and how they move: solid, liquid, and gas.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Each state has its own shape, volume, and particle behavior, but all matter is made of the same kinds of tiny particles — what changes between the states is how closely packed those particles are and how much energy they have.

2. Particle arrangement in solids, liquids, and gases
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] The image shows three arrangements of particles representing solids, liquids, and gases. In the first, the solid shows particles tightly packed together in fixed positions, suggesting a rigid structure. The second arrangement depicts particles close together but able to move, indicating a fluid state. Finally, the third shows particles far apart and moving freely, representing a gaseous state where movement is unrestricted.

3. As a general rule
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] As a general rule: the more energy particles have, the faster they move and the further apart they spread. Solids have the least particle energy and movement; gases have the most.

4. Matter
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. A book, water, air, a spoon, and a balloon all contain matter. Matter can be described using observable properties such as shape, volume, texture, color, and how it behaves inside a container.

5. Solid
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume. Its particles are packed closely together and mainly vibrate in fixed positions. Examples include a stone, pencil, wooden block, and ice cube.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] In a solid, particles are packed tightly together in a fixed, orderly pattern. They vibrate in place but do not move past one another.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] particles in a solid are drawn as evenly spaced dots arranged in tidy rows and columns, like a grid, showing a fixed, orderly pattern.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Has a fixed shape -- it does not change shape unless a force is applied. Has a fixed volume -- it takes up the same amount of space no matter what container it is in. Particles are tightly packed and only vibrate in place. Generally difficult to compress (squeeze into a smaller space). Everyday examples: ice cubes, a wooden chair, a rock, a coin, and a book.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] In a solid, particles are packed tightly together in a fixed pattern. They vibrate in place but do not move past one another. Because of this, a solid keeps a definite shape and a definite volume — it does not change shape to fit a container.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Everyday examples: An ice cube keeps its shape whether it sits in a bowl or on a plate. A wooden block stays the same size and shape no matter where you put it. A rock does not flow or spread out like a liquid would. Key idea: solid particles are held in place by strong forces between them, so heating a solid makes the particles vibrate faster until, at the melting point, they break free of their fixed positions and the solid becomes a liquid.

6. Liquid
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape. It flows and takes the shape of its container. Its particles are close together but can slide past one another. Examples include water, milk, and cooking oil.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] In a liquid, particles are still close together, but they have more energy than in a solid. They can slide past one another, which is why liquids can flow and change shape.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] particles in a liquid are drawn as dots that are close together but scattered in an irregular, flowing arrangement rather than a neat grid, showing that they can move around each other.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Has no fixed shape -- it takes the shape of its container. Has a fixed volume -- the amount of liquid stays the same even if the container changes. Particles can slide past each other, allowing the liquid to flow. Generally difficult to compress, though slightly easier than a solid. Everyday examples: water, milk, cooking oil, and juice.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] In a liquid, particles are still close together, but they are free to slide past one another. This gives a liquid a definite volume but no fixed shape — it takes the shape of whatever container holds it, while the amount of liquid stays the same.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Everyday examples: Water poured from a bottle into a cup changes shape but not amount. Juice flows and settles at the bottom of any container you pour it into. Cooking oil spreads out flat when poured onto a pan. Key idea: heating a liquid gives its particles enough energy to escape into the air entirely, a process called evaporation, which turns the liquid into a gas at its boiling point.

7. Gas
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume. It spreads out and fills the available space in its container. Its particles are far apart and move freely. Air and water vapor are examples of gases.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] In a gas, particles have the most energy of the three states. They move quickly in every direction and spread far apart, filling all the space available to them.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] particles in a gas are drawn as widely spaced dots, each with a short line beside it representing fast, free movement in many directions.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Has no fixed shape and no fixed volume -- it spreads out to fill its container completely. Particles move quickly and freely, with large spaces between them. Easy to compress because there is so much empty space between particles. Everyday examples: the air around you, helium in a balloon, and steam rising from a hot drink.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] In a gas, particles are spread far apart and move quickly in every direction. A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume — it expands to completely fill whatever container it is in.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Everyday examples: The air inside a balloon spreads out evenly to fill the whole balloon. Steam rising from a hot cup of tea spreads out into the room. A helium tank fills every party balloon you attach to it, one at a time. Key idea: cooling a gas slows its particles down until they pack close enough together to form a liquid again, a process called condensation.

9. Comparing the Three States
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] Solids keep their shape; liquids and gases take the shape of their container.
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] Solids and liquids have definite volume; gases expand to fill available space.
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] Solid particles are tightly packed, liquid particles are close but mobile, and gas particles are widely spaced.
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] Liquids and gases can flow, while solids normally do not.

10. Changing From One State to Another
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Adding or removing heat energy can change matter from one state to another. These changes are called changes of state. The names below describe the direction of each change.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Matter can change from one state to another when heat energy is added or removed. Adding heat makes particles move faster and spread apart (melting, then evaporation). Removing heat makes particles slow down and move closer together (condensation, then freezing). These changes are physical changes: the substance is still the same material, just arranged differently. Melting — solid to liquid, caused by adding heat. Freezing — liquid to solid, caused by removing heat. Evaporation — liquid to gas, caused by adding heat. Condensation — gas to liquid, caused by removing heat.

11. 6. Changing From One State to Another
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] The figure shows a table outlining changes between states of matter. It lists four changes: melting, freezing, evaporating, and condensing. The first entry describes melting, showing a change from a solid to a liquid, and indicates that energy is added, causing the particles to move faster and spread out. The second entry details freezing, where a liquid transforms into a solid, and energy is removed, resulting in the particles slowing down and locking into place. The third entry explains evaporating or boiling, where a liquid becomes a gas, and energy is added, allowing the particles to move fast enough to escape and spread far apart. Finally, the fourth entry describes condensing, where a gas turns back into a liquid, and energy is removed, causing the particles to slow down and move closer together.

14. Summary: what to remember (Part 1 of 2)
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Matter is made of particles and commonly exists as a solid, liquid, or gas. Solids hold a fixed shape and volume because their particles are tightly packed. Liquids keep a fixed volume but flow to fit their container because their particles can slide past each other.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Gases have no fixed shape or volume because their particles move quickly and spread far apart. Adding or removing heat energy can change matter from one state to another through melting, freezing, evaporating, and condensing.

15. Everyday Examples
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] An ice cube is a solid, melted ice is liquid water, and water vapor is a gas. These examples show that the same substance can exist in different states. Heating and cooling can cause changes of state, but understanding each state should come first.

## Grouping Materials Based on Properties (topic 341)

1. The three classification tests specified in the Grade 4 competency
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] The figure shows three separate experiments designed to classify materials. The first experiment displays two materials: absorbent cloth, paper, and sponge on the left, and non-absorbent plastic, glass, and metal on the right. The second experiment shows a wooden and plastic float alongside a stone and metal sink, with the materials placed in water to observe whether they float or sink. Finally, the third experiment presents two groups of materials: decaying leaves, food scraps, paper, and wood on one side, and materials that do not decay – plastic, glass, metal, and rubber – on the other.

2. Properties of Materials (Part 1 of 3)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] Every material can be described by the way it looks, feels, and behaves. These descriptions are its properties. When learners in the elementary grades sort materials, they are not memorizing lists — they are using a property as a rule for putting things into groups.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] This skill is introduced in Grade 3 with the physical properties of solid materials, and is extended in Grade 4 into three specific classification tests that learners can actually perform in the classroom. Physical properties can be observed without changing what the material is made of.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] The Grade 3 competency names three that learners can check by hand: whether a material is hard (a stone, a coin), shiny (a new spoon, aluminum foil, a tansan), or stretchable (a rubber band, a garter, a balloon). Other familiar physical properties are color, texture, size, weight, and flexibility. Chemical properties describe how a material behaves when it reacts or changes into something new.

3. The Grade 4 competency names three
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] The Grade 4 competency names three: whether a material can be burned, whether it reacts with other materials, and whether it is degradable or biodegradable. These properties cannot be seen at a glance; they show themselves only when the material is exposed to fire, air, moisture, or other substances.

4. Ability to absorb water. Place a drop of water on the material and watch what happens. If the
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] water soaks in and the material becomes damp, the material is absorbent. If the water stays on the surface as a bead and can be wiped off, the material is non-absorbent. Cloth, paper, sponge, cotton, and wood absorb water; plastic, glass, metal, and rubber do not.

5. Ability to float or sink. Place the material gently in a basin of water. Materials that stay on the
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] surface float; materials that go down to the bottom sink. Dry wood, plastic bottle caps, styrofoam, and dry leaves float. Stone, coins, nails, and marbles sink. Learners often expect size to decide this, so it is worth showing them a large piece of wood that floats and a small nail that sinks.

6. Ability to undergo decay. Materials that rot, break down, and return to the soil over time are said
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] to undergo decay, or to be biodegradable. Food scraps, leaves, paper, and wood decay. Plastic, glass, metal, and rubber do not decay within a useful length of time, so they are called non- biodegradable. This test cannot be finished in one class period; it is usually set up as a week-long or month-long observation using two buried samples.

7. Grouping the Same Materials in Different Ways
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] There is no single correct way to group a set of materials. The grouping depends on which property is being used as the rule. Consider six items commonly available in a Filipino classroom: Reading the table down each column gives a different pair of groups each time. The basahan joins the dried leaf and the wood under one rule, but joins the plastic bag under another. This is the central idea of the lesson: a material has several properties at once, and the group it belongs to changes with the question being asked.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] The table shows six different materials arranged in three columns. The first column lists the materials: basahan, plastic sando bag, dried leaf, tansan, piece of wood, and marble. The second and third columns describe their properties in relation to water and decay. The basahan floats at first, then sinks, and if made of cotton, it undergoes decay. The plastic sando bag floats and does not undergo decay. The dried leaf floats and does undergo decay, and the tansan, which is a bottle cap, sinks and does not undergo decay.

8. Why Grouping Matters (Part 1 of 3)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] Use, Handling, and Disposal People choose materials for a purpose because of their properties. A raincoat and a payong are made of plastic or coated cloth because those materials do not absorb water. A bimpo and a basahan are made of cotton because cotton does.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] A banca is made of wood because wood floats. Knowing the property explains the choice. Grouping also decides how waste should be handled, which is the point of the Grade 3 competency on proper disposal. Biodegradable waste such as vegetable peelings and fallen leaves can be composted and returned to the garden as fertilizer.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties] Non-biodegradable waste such as plastic bottles, tin cans, and glass must be cleaned and set aside for recycling, because it will not break down on its own. This is the science behind the familiar nabubulok and di-nabubulok labels on segregation bins in schools and barangays.
