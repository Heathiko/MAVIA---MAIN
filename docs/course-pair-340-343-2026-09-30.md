## Solid, Liquid and Gas (topic 340)

1. Matter usually exists in one of three everyday states
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Matter usually exists in one of three everyday states: solid, liquid, and gas. What state matter is in depends on two things: how closely packed its particles are, and how much energy (movement) those particles have.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. Your desk, the air you breathe, the water you drink, and even you are all made of matter. Scientists sort matter into three common states based on how its particles are arranged and how they move: solid, liquid, and gas.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Each state has its own shape, volume, and particle behavior, but all matter is made of the same kinds of tiny particles — what changes between the states is how closely packed those particles are and how much energy they have.

3. As a general rule
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] As a general rule: the more energy particles have, the faster they move and the further apart they spread. Solids have the least particle energy and movement; gases have the most.

4. Matter
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. A book, water, air, a spoon, and a balloon all contain matter. Matter can be described using observable properties such as shape, volume, texture, color, and how it behaves inside a container.

5. Solid
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume. Its particles are packed closely together and mainly vibrate in fixed positions. Examples include a stone, pencil, wooden block, and ice cube.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] In a solid, particles are packed tightly together in a fixed, orderly pattern. They vibrate in place but do not move past one another.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] particles in a solid are drawn as evenly spaced dots arranged in tidy rows and columns, like a grid, showing a fixed, orderly pattern.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Has a fixed shape -- it does not change shape unless a force is applied. Has a fixed volume -- it takes up the same amount of space no matter what container it is in. Particles are tightly packed and only vibrate in place. Generally difficult to compress (squeeze into a smaller space). Everyday examples: ice cubes, a wooden chair, a rock, a coin, and a book.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] In a solid, particles are packed tightly together in a fixed pattern. They vibrate in place but do not move past one another. Because of this, a solid keeps a definite shape and a definite volume — it does not change shape to fit a container.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Everyday examples: An ice cube keeps its shape whether it sits in a bowl or on a plate. A wooden block stays the same size and shape no matter where you put it. A rock does not flow or spread out like a liquid would. Key idea: solid particles are held in place by strong forces between them, so heating a solid makes the particles vibrate faster until, at the melting point, they break free of their fixed positions and the solid becomes a liquid.

6. Liquid
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape. It flows and takes the shape of its container. Its particles are close together but can slide past one another. Examples include water, milk, and cooking oil.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] In a liquid, particles are still close together, but they have more energy than in a solid. They can slide past one another, which is why liquids can flow and change shape.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] particles in a liquid are drawn as dots that are close together but scattered in an irregular, flowing arrangement rather than a neat grid, showing that they can move around each other.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Has no fixed shape -- it takes the shape of its container. Has a fixed volume -- the amount of liquid stays the same even if the container changes. Particles can slide past each other, allowing the liquid to flow. Generally difficult to compress, though slightly easier than a solid. Everyday examples: water, milk, cooking oil, and juice.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] In a liquid, particles are still close together, but they are free to slide past one another. This gives a liquid a definite volume but no fixed shape — it takes the shape of whatever container holds it, while the amount of liquid stays the same.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Everyday examples: Water poured from a bottle into a cup changes shape but not amount. Juice flows and settles at the bottom of any container you pour it into. Cooking oil spreads out flat when poured onto a pan. Key idea: heating a liquid gives its particles enough energy to escape into the air entirely, a process called evaporation, which turns the liquid into a gas at its boiling point.

7. Gas
   - [Lesson-1_Solid-Liquid-and-Gas-1] A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume. It spreads out and fills the available space in its container. Its particles are far apart and move freely. Air and water vapor are examples of gases.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] In a gas, particles have the most energy of the three states. They move quickly in every direction and spread far apart, filling all the space available to them.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] particles in a gas are drawn as widely spaced dots, each with a short line beside it representing fast, free movement in many directions.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Has no fixed shape and no fixed volume -- it spreads out to fill its container completely. Particles move quickly and freely, with large spaces between them. Easy to compress because there is so much empty space between particles. Everyday examples: the air around you, helium in a balloon, and steam rising from a hot drink.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] In a gas, particles are spread far apart and move quickly in every direction. A gas has no definite shape and no definite volume — it expands to completely fill whatever container it is in.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Everyday examples: The air inside a balloon spreads out evenly to fill the whole balloon. Steam rising from a hot cup of tea spreads out into the room. A helium tank fills every party balloon you attach to it, one at a time. Key idea: cooling a gas slows its particles down until they pack close enough together to form a liquid again, a process called condensation.

10. Changing From One State to Another
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Adding or removing heat energy can change matter from one state to another. These changes are called changes of state. The names below describe the direction of each change.
   - [Solid_Liquid_and_Gas] Matter can change from one state to another when heat energy is added or removed. Adding heat makes particles move faster and spread apart (melting, then evaporation). Removing heat makes particles slow down and move closer together (condensation, then freezing). These changes are physical changes: the substance is still the same material, just arranged differently. Melting — solid to liquid, caused by adding heat. Freezing — liquid to solid, caused by removing heat. Evaporation — liquid to gas, caused by adding heat. Condensation — gas to liquid, caused by removing heat.

14. Summary: what to remember (Part 1 of 2)
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Matter is made of particles and commonly exists as a solid, liquid, or gas. Solids hold a fixed shape and volume because their particles are tightly packed. Liquids keep a fixed volume but flow to fit their container because their particles can slide past each other.
   - [Solid-Liquid-Gas-2] Gases have no fixed shape or volume because their particles move quickly and spread far apart. Adding or removing heat energy can change matter from one state to another through melting, freezing, evaporating, and condensing.

15. Everyday Examples

1. Solutions, colloids, and suspensions differ in appearance and in how they treat light

2. Uniform and Non-Uniform Mixtures (Part 1 of 3)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] The Grade 6 competency asks learners to sort mixtures by appearance. The question is simple: does A uniform mixture, also called a homogeneous mixture, looks the same in every part. The components are so evenly spread that you cannot tell them apart by looking.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] A glass of sugar water, a cup of brewed coffee without grounds, and a sip of buko juice all look like a single liquid, even though each one contains several materials. Uniform mixtures of this kind are called solutions. A non-uniform mixture, also called a heterogeneous mixture, shows its components.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] You can see the separate parts, and different spoonfuls may not look the same. A bowl of halo-halo, a plate of pancit, a handful of rice mixed with corn, and a glass of muddy water are all non-uniform.

3. Solutions: Solute and Solvent
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] In a solution, the material that dissolves is the solute, and the material that does the dissolving is the solvent. In sweetened coffee, the sugar is the solute and the hot water is the solvent. The solvent is usually the component present in the greater amount, and water is so common as a solvent that it is often called the universal solvent. Three conditions affect how quickly a solute dissolves, and learners can test each one: Size of the particles. Finely ground sugar dissolves faster than a large lump, because more of its surface touches the water. Stirring. A stirred glass dissolves its sugar faster than one left standing, because stirring keeps bringing fresh water into contact with the solute. Temperature. Sugar dissolves faster in hot water than in cold water, which is why powdered drinks mix easily in warm water but leave grains at the bottom of a cold glass. Not every solute dissolves in every solvent. Salt and sugar dissolve in water, but cooking oil does not. Oil and water separate into two layers no matter how long they are stirred, which is why a bottle of salad dressing must be shaken before use.

4. Suspensions and Colloids (Part 1 of 3)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Between the clear solution and the obviously separated mixture are two kinds worth naming. A suspension is a non-uniform mixture in which solid particles are large enough to be seen and heavy enough to settle when the mixture is left alone.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Gawgaw stirred into water, muddy water from a flooded street, and a bottle of medicine labelled "shake well before using" are all suspensions. Their particles can be trapped by filter paper. A colloid sits in between.

5. Air as a Mixture
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Not all mixtures are liquids. Air is a mixture of gases, and the Grade 6 competency names its main components: nitrogen, which makes up the largest share; oxygen, which living things need for respiration; carbon dioxide, which plants use to make food; and water vapor, whose amount rises and falls with the weather.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Air is a uniform mixture: it looks the same throughout, and its gases do not settle into layers. Because the components are only physically combined, each keeps its own properties — which is why oxygen can still support burning even while it is mixed with the other gases.
