2. Properties of Materials (Part 1 of 3)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo830 | text] Properties of Materials (Part 1 of 3)
     Every material can be described by the way it looks, feels, and behaves. These descriptions are its properties. When learners in the elementary grades sort materials, they are not memorizing lists — they are using a property as a rule for putting things into groups.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo831 | text] Properties of Materials (Part 2 of 3)
     This skill is introduced in Grade 3 with the physical properties of solid materials, and is extended in Grade 4 into three specific classification tests that learners can actually perform in the classroom. Physical properties can be observed without changing what the material is made of.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo832 | text] Properties of Materials (Part 3 of 3)
     The Grade 3 competency names three that learners can check by hand: whether a material is hard (a stone, a coin), shiny (a new spoon, aluminum foil, a tansan), or stretchable (a rubber band, a garter, a balloon). Other familiar physical properties are color, texture, size, weight, and flexibility. Chemical properties describe how a material behaves when it reacts or changes into something new.

3. The Grade 4 competency names three
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo833 | text] The Grade 4 competency names three
     The Grade 4 competency names three: whether a material can be burned, whether it reacts with other materials, and whether it is degradable or biodegradable. These properties cannot be seen at a glance; they show themselves only when the material is exposed to fire, air, moisture, or other substances.

4. Ability to absorb water. Place a drop of water on the material and watch what happens. If the
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo834 | text] Ability to absorb water. Place a drop of water on the material and watch what happens. If the
     water soaks in and the material becomes damp, the material is absorbent. If the water stays on the surface as a bead and can be wiped off, the material is non-absorbent. Cloth, paper, sponge, cotton, and wood absorb water; plastic, glass, metal, and rubber do not.

5. Ability to float or sink. Place the material gently in a basin of water. Materials that stay on the
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo835 | text] Ability to float or sink. Place the material gently in a basin of water. Materials that stay on the
     surface float; materials that go down to the bottom sink. Dry wood, plastic bottle caps, styrofoam, and dry leaves float. Stone, coins, nails, and marbles sink. Learners often expect size to decide this, so it is worth showing them a large piece of wood that floats and a small nail that sinks.

6. Ability to undergo decay. Materials that rot, break down, and return to the soil over time are said
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo836 | text] Ability to undergo decay. Materials that rot, break down, and return to the soil over time are said
     to undergo decay, or to be biodegradable. Food scraps, leaves, paper, and wood decay. Plastic, glass, metal, and rubber do not decay within a useful length of time, so they are called non- biodegradable. This test cannot be finished in one class period; it is usually set up as a week-long or month-long observation using two buried samples.

7. Grouping the Same Materials in Different Ways
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo837 | text] Grouping the Same Materials in Different Ways
     There is no single correct way to group a set of materials. The grouping depends on which property is being used as the rule. Consider six items commonly available in a Filipino classroom: Reading the table down each column gives a different pair of groups each time. The basahan joins the dried leaf and the wood under one rule, but joins the plastic bag under another. This is the central idea of the lesson: a material has several properties at once, and the group it belongs to changes with the question being asked.

8. Why Grouping Matters (Part 1 of 3)
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo839 | text] Why Grouping Matters (Part 1 of 3)
     Use, Handling, and Disposal People choose materials for a purpose because of their properties. A raincoat and a payong are made of plastic or coated cloth because those materials do not absorb water. A bimpo and a basahan are made of cotton because cotton does.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo840 | text] Why Grouping Matters (Part 2 of 3)
     A banca is made of wood because wood floats. Knowing the property explains the choice. Grouping also decides how waste should be handled, which is the point of the Grade 3 competency on proper disposal. Biodegradable waste such as vegetable peelings and fallen leaves can be composted and returned to the garden as fertilizer.
   - [Lesson_Grouping-Materials-Based-on-Properties | lo841 | text] Why Grouping Matters (Part 3 of 3)
     Non-biodegradable waste such as plastic bottles, tin cans, and glass must be cleaned and set aside for recycling, because it will not break down on its own. This is the science behind the familiar nabubulok and di-nabubulok labels on segregation bins in schools and barangays.
