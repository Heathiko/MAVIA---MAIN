## Mixtures and Their Characteristics (topic 343)

1. Solutions, colloids, and suspensions differ in appearance and in how they treat light
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] The illustration shows three distinct types of mixtures: a solution, a colloid, and a suspension. In the first image, a solution is depicted as a uniform and clear liquid, with light passing straight through it and the particles within it being too small to be seen or settle. The second image shows a colloid, where light is scattered inside the mixture, and the particles do not separate when the mixture is left undisturbed, like gatas, taho, or mayonesa. Finally, the third image represents a suspension, which is cloudy and the solid particles settle at the bottom when it is left undisturbed, such as muddy water or gawgaw in water, and light is blocked.

2. Uniform and Non-Uniform Mixtures (Part 1 of 3)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] The Grade 6 competency asks learners to sort mixtures by appearance. The question is simple: does A uniform mixture, also called a homogeneous mixture, looks the same in every part. The components are so evenly spread that you cannot tell them apart by looking.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] A glass of sugar water, a cup of brewed coffee without grounds, and a sip of buko juice all look like a single liquid, even though each one contains several materials. Uniform mixtures of this kind are called solutions. A non-uniform mixture, also called a heterogeneous mixture, shows its components.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] You can see the separate parts, and different spoonfuls may not look the same. A bowl of halo-halo, a plate of pancit, a handful of rice mixed with corn, and a glass of muddy water are all non-uniform.

3. Solutions: Solute and Solvent
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] In a solution, the material that dissolves is the solute, and the material that does the dissolving is the solvent. In sweetened coffee, the sugar is the solute and the hot water is the solvent. The solvent is usually the component present in the greater amount, and water is so common as a solvent that it is often called the universal solvent. Three conditions affect how quickly a solute dissolves, and learners can test each one: Size of the particles. Finely ground sugar dissolves faster than a large lump, because more of its surface touches the water. Stirring. A stirred glass dissolves its sugar faster than one left standing, because stirring keeps bringing fresh water into contact with the solute. Temperature. Sugar dissolves faster in hot water than in cold water, which is why powdered drinks mix easily in warm water but leave grains at the bottom of a cold glass. Not every solute dissolves in every solvent. Salt and sugar dissolve in water, but cooking oil does not. Oil and water separate into two layers no matter how long they are stirred, which is why a bottle of salad dressing must be shaken before use.

4. Suspensions and Colloids (Part 1 of 3)
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Between the clear solution and the obviously separated mixture are two kinds worth naming. A suspension is a non-uniform mixture in which solid particles are large enough to be seen and heavy enough to settle when the mixture is left alone.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Gawgaw stirred into water, muddy water from a flooded street, and a bottle of medicine labelled "shake well before using" are all suspensions. Their particles can be trapped by filter paper. A colloid sits in between.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Its particles are too small to settle out, so the mixture stays evenly cloudy for a long time, but they are still large enough to scatter light. 5. Air as a Mixture
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Not all mixtures are liquids. Air is a mixture of gases, and the Grade 6 competency names its main components: nitrogen, which makes up the largest share; oxygen, which living things need for respiration; carbon dioxide, which plants use to make food; and water vapor, whose amount rises and falls with the weather.
   - [Lesson_Mixtures-and-Their-Characteristics] Air is a uniform mixture: it looks the same throughout, and its gases do not settle into layers. Because the components are only physically combined, each keeps its own properties — which is why oxygen can still support burning even while it is mixed with the other gases.

## Separating Mixture (topic 348)

1. Each technique takes advantage of a different property of the components
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] The figure shows four different methods for separating mixtures. First, there’s decantation, which demonstrates pouring off the clear liquid, allowing heavier solids to settle at the bottom. Second, filtering shows a container with a filter that traps solids but allows the liquid to pass through. Third, evaporation is depicted with a container of water that is heating up, causing the water to leave as vapor and leaving the dissolved solid behind. Finally, scooping is shown with a container where one part of the mixture floats on top, and you lift that floating layer.

2. Why Mixtures Can Be Separated
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] Because the components of a mixture are only physically combined, each one keeps its own properties.

3. Picking is the simplest method. The components differ in appearance and are large enough to be
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] handled one at a time. Removing small stones from rice before cooking, taking the bones out of fried fish, and sorting good mongo seeds from damaged ones are all examples of picking.

4. Sieving separates solids of different particle sizes using a sieve, strainer, or screen. The finer
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] particles fall through the holes while the larger ones stay behind. Sifting flour to remove lumps, screening sand at a construction site, and straining brewed tea leaves from the liquid all use sieving.

5. Winnowing separates light components from heavy ones using moving air. Farmers toss threshed
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] rice into the air using a bilao; the wind carries away the light husks or ipa while the heavier grains fall back into the tray. The same principle is used when separating chaff from mongo or corn.

6. Scooping removes a component that has floated to the surface. Skimming the fat from a pot of
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] sabaw, lifting oil off the top of water, and gathering leaves from the surface of a pail all use scooping.

7. Decantation separates a liquid from a solid that has settled, or two liquids that do not mix. The
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] mixture is left to stand until the heavier component sinks, then the upper liquid is poured off carefully without disturbing the layer below. Rinsing rice, letting muddy water settle before pouring off the clear part, and separating oil from water all use decantation.

8. Filtering uses a filter paper, cloth, or fine mesh through which the liquid passes while the solid is
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] trapped. It catches particles too fine to remove by decantation. Brewing coffee through a cloth strainer, straining gata from grated coconut, and using a household water filter are all examples.

9. Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid from a solution by heating the liquid or leaving it under the
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] sun until the liquid escapes as vapor, leaving the solid behind. This is exactly how asin is produced in the salt beds of Pangasinan and other coastal provinces: seawater is led into shallow ponds, the sun evaporates the water, and salt crystals remain.

10. Using a magnet separates iron or steel from other materials, because only certain metals are
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] attracted to magnets. A magnet passed over a mixture of iron filings and sand lifts out the iron and leaves the sand. Junk shops and recycling facilities use large magnets to pull steel out of mixed scrap.

11. Choosing the Right Technique
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] Some mixtures need more than one technique used in order. To recover both the salt and the sand from a mixture of salt, sand, and water, a learner would first filter the mixture to trap the sand, then evaporate the filtered liquid to recover the salt. Planning the correct sequence is the most demanding part of this competency.

12. Choosing the Right Technique
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] The figure shows a table outlining techniques for separating mixtures. The first entry details rice mixed with small stones, where the differing property is appearance and size, and the best technique is picking up the stones. The second entry shows flour with lumps, where the differing property is particle size, and the best technique is sieving the flour to remove the lumps. The third entry describes palay mixed with husks, where the differing property is weight, and the best technique is winnowing, which involves tossing the mixture to allow the lighter husks to blow away. The fourth entry details sabaw with fat on top, where the differing property is the fat’s position, and the best technique is scooping the fat from the top. The fifth entry shows sand and water, where the sand settles, and the best technique is decantation, which involves carefully pouring off the water leaving the sand behind.

13. Benefits of Separating Mixtures (Part 1 of 2)
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] Separation is not a classroom exercise only; it is how many useful products are made. It gives us clean drinking water from cloudy sources, salt from seawater, gata from grated coconut, cooking oil from crushed seeds, and clean rice ready for the pot.
   - [Lesson_Separating-Mixtures] It allows recyclable metal to be recovered from mixed waste, and it is the reason a school can turn segregated waste into something valuable. In every case, separating a mixture takes something useful out of something that was not yet usable.
